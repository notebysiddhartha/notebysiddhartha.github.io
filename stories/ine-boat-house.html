The House That Made Room for a Boat

Ine, Kyoto · August 2026
A boat house in Ine.

Ine had looked beautiful in photographs, but walking through the village felt very different.

The road ran quietly alongside the sea, and the Funaya seemed to sit between the land and the water, almost as if they were floating.

As we walked, we came across one of the boat houses that was open to visitors. An old woman was sitting there, handing out tickets with a warm smile.

What struck me was not the ticket or even the house, but the happiness on her face as she welcomed people in and showed them around. She seemed genuinely pleased that visitors had come to see a small part of the life of her village.

Inside, I found something I hadn't quite expected.

There was a boat inside the house.

I stood there for a moment, looking at it. The water came right into the lower part of the building, and the boat rested there as naturally as a car might sit in a garage.

And suddenly, the house made sense.

It wasn't simply an unusual or beautiful piece of architecture. The house had been shaped around the life of the people who lived there. The boat was not kept separate from the home.

The house had made room for it.

It reminded me of India.

Growing up, I have often seen houses that quietly reveal the lives of the people who live in them. A small shop becoming part of the front room, a verandah becoming a workplace, tools and bicycles finding their permanent place in the house.

The boundaries between living and working are often quite fluid. The house adapts to the life around it.

In Ine, I saw something similar in a completely different setting.

Here, the sea had shaped the house.

Later, sitting on tatami mats and eating a traditional Japanese meal, I found myself thinking about that boat and the old woman at the entrance.

Perhaps what stayed with me most was that both seemed to represent the same thing—the way a place carries its history not through grand monuments, but through ordinary things and the people who continue to care for them.

The boat was still there.
The house was still there.
And the old woman was still happily opening its doors to strangers.

Perhaps that is what makes a place feel alive.

Not simply that it has preserved its past, but that someone still smiles when they show it to you.
